Book One: The Age of Peace

Two brothers. One promise made at a kitchen table in Lilburn, Georgia. And a war nobody else could see.

The story

Cameron Thorne was sixteen when a history teacher's tape about a brother who vanished in Cuba for writing a letter changed his life. His little brother Eli was ten and already taking apart anything with wires. That night they pinned a map of Havana to the table and promised each other they would never just watch.

Twenty years later, Cameron is the blade and Eli is the mind in the walls. Their quiet security company has grown into a network of people who keep the lights on when governments fail. Then a senator is shot in front of them in Washington, a staged crisis built to sell the country a new kind of peace, and the brothers follow the money to the Concord: a secret coalition that rules by engineering fear.

From Atlanta to Havana to Madrid and back to the Capitol, Cameron and Eli fight a silent war against an enemy that would rather recruit them than kill them. Every victory costs blood. And the closer they come to winning, the harder one question gets: how do you break a machine built to control the world without becoming it?

The Architects is a fast, cinematic thriller about brotherhood, power and the price of freedom, and the first book in a series.

Chapter 1

Lilburn

The Thorne house sat off Lawrenceville Highway in a pocket of Lilburn that looked too ordinary to ever make history. Vinyl siding. Mailboxes with little red flags. The hum of highway tires drifting in like ocean noise.

Cameron Thorne liked the hour after dinner, when the dishes were stacked and the TV in the living room played low enough to feel like a second heartbeat. Sixteen and built lean from football and pull-ups, he sat at the dining table with a world history book open, but his eyes kept sliding toward his little brother.

Eli was ten and already impatient with anything that couldn’t be taken apart. A dead Gateway tower lay on the floor beside him, its metal skin peeled back like a tin can. Wires, screws, a motherboard, and a soldering iron their dad trusted him with, because Eli never treated tools like toys, were spread across a towel.

“You’re gonna cook it,” Cameron said, not looking up.

Eli didn’t glance up either. “Already did. I’m trying to reverse it.”

Cameron’s mouth twitched. He turned a page like he meant it. “Of course you are.”

From the living room came a voice, not their father’s, not the news anchor’s, something sharper, accented, and tired. Cameron had brought home a VHS from school: a recorded guest lecture his history teacher had delivered to a civic group. His parents had agreed to watch “a few minutes.” Now the tape ran mostly as background noise.

“…they told us it was peace,” the voice said. “But peace that is forced is not peace. It is a cage.”

Eli finally looked up, eyes narrowing. “Is that your teacher?”

“Mr. Emayonase,” Cameron said. He’d never heard a man speak about history like it was still bleeding. “He left Cuba when he was seventeen. His family didn’t get out.”

On the TV, Mr. Emayonase paused. You could hear the room, the coughs, the cheap folding chairs. “My brother disappeared,” he said softly. “He wrote a letter. A letter. That’s all. One day he went to the corner store and did not come back.”

Eli’s lips pressed together. The soldering iron hissed as he set it down. “That’s messed up.”

Cameron closed his book. He didn’t mean to. His hands did it on their own.

Outside the window, Lilburn glowed the way suburbs always did, porch lights, a dog barking once and deciding it wasn’t worth it, a passing car’s headlights washing across a row of quiet yards. Stone Mountain wasn’t far, but you couldn’t see it from here. The world felt small and safe.

“It’s not fair,” Cameron said. His voice came out quieter than he intended. “We live off Rockbridge Road and worry about tests. And other people…” He gestured at the TV like he could point to an island through drywall. “They whisper to survive.”

Eli picked at a wire end, focusing hard the way he always did when he was scared. “What would you do?”

Cameron stared at him. “What?”

Eli’s eyes were bright, not teary, Eli didn’t do tears if he could help it, but bright like a lens catching sun. “If you could. What would you do?”

Cameron’s thoughts drifted to a field trip the year before, a drive past factories and rail lines and the kind of neighborhoods adults called “rough” without ever stopping. Power, he realized, wasn’t only guns or flags. Power was who controlled the lights, the water, the money, the stories.

He reached into his backpack and pulled out a folded sheet of paper. Not homework. A printout map, Havana, grainy, old, marked with tiny street names that made the place feel real instead of mythical.

He smoothed it on the table between them. “I don’t know,” he said. Then he lied less. “I know I don’t want to just watch.”

Eli leaned in. “Why do you have that?”

“Because I can’t get his voice out of my head,” Cameron admitted. He tapped a neighborhood near the center. “Because I keep thinking…if someone could build something stronger than governments, something that doesn’t rot every four years…”

Eli’s eyebrows rose. “Like a company?”

Cameron blinked. “Yeah.”

“A company can still be bad,” Eli said, but it wasn’t doubt. It was the way Eli tested everything for weak points before he trusted it.

“Then we build one that isn’t,” Cameron said.

From the hallway, their dad called, “You boys alright?”

“Yeah!” Eli answered fast.

Cameron added, softer, “We’re good.”

Eli reached into the junk pile and pulled out a thumbtack from a corkboard he’d stolen for parts. He held it out like an offering.

Cameron took it. For a second, he felt ridiculous, two kids at a kitchen table in Gwinnett County, planning to fix the world. Then he remembered Mr. Emayonase’s voice: a letter. A corner store. A life erased.

He pressed the tack into the paper, dead center of Havana.

Eli watched it go in. “So what’s the plan?”

Cameron met his brother’s gaze. He didn’t say “someday” or “maybe.” He didn’t soften it for a ten-year-old.

“We learn,” he said. “We train. We build. And when we’re old enough…we stop asking permission.”

Eli nodded once. A small nod, but it felt like a lock clicking shut.

The TV kept talking about cages and freedom. Outside, a car passed on Lawrenceville Highway. The porch light across the street flicked off.

And in that quiet Georgia house, with solder smoke in the air and an island map pinned to a table, the first brick of the Architects was laid.
